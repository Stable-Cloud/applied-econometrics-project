## Test 1: VIF Test (Variance Inflation Factor)

### Purpose
The VIF test assesses multicollinearity among the independent variables in a regression model. High VIF values indicate that an independent variable is highly correlated with other independent variables, violating the assumption of no perfect multicollinearity and inflating the variance of coefficient estimates.

### Rule of Thumb
- VIF < 5 → **No concern**
- VIF 5–10 → **Moderate multicollinearity**
- VIF > 10 → **Severe multicollinearity**

For models with categorical variables and interactions, the Generalized VIF (GVIF) scaled to $\text{GVIF}^{1/(2 \cdot Df)}$ is used for comparability.

**This is a critical advantage of M13 over Model B (Comprehensive Interaction), which had a Max VIF of 115.19 — indicating catastrophic multicollinearity.**

## Test 2: Breusch-Godfrey Test (BG Test)

### Purpose
The Breusch-Godfrey test checks for autocorrelation up to a certain lag order in the residuals of a regression model. Unlike the Durbin-Watson test, it can test for higher-order autocorrelation and is valid in the presence of lagged dependent variables.

### Hypotheses
- **H₀:** There is no autocorrelation up to the specified lag order among the residuals.
- **H₁:** There is autocorrelation up to the specified lag order.

### Results

| Lag Order | LM Statistic (χ²) | p-value | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3,085.19 | < 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶ | Reject H₀ |
| 2 | 4,358.37 | < 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶ | Reject H₀ |
| 4 | 6,063.29 | < 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶ | Reject H₀ |

### Verdict

| | |
|---|---|
| **Result** | **Reject H₀** (statistically) |
| Practical significance | **Negligible** |

The BG test formally rejects the null at all lag orders. However, this must be interpreted in context:

1. **Sample size inflation:** With N = 480,052 observations, the BG test has enormous statistical power. Even trivially small correlations (e.g., ρ ≈ 0.001) will produce highly significant test statistics. The test does not distinguish between statistically significant and practically meaningful autocorrelation.

2. **Cross-sectional data:** Autocorrelation is primarily a concern in time-series data where observations have a natural ordering. In cross-sectional data, the ordering of observations is arbitrary (determined by the survey enumeration), and "autocorrelation" reflects spatial clustering rather than temporal dependence.

3. **Mitigation in M13:** Model M13 uses **cluster-robust standard errors** at the PSU level, which explicitly account for within-cluster correlation in the residuals. This is the appropriate correction for the NFHS survey design and renders the standard autocorrelation tests less relevant.

## Test 3: Durbin-Watson Test

### Purpose
The Durbin-Watson test checks for first-order autocorrelation in the residuals of a regression model. The test statistic ranges from 0 to 4, where a value near 2 indicates no autocorrelation.

### Hypotheses
- **H₀:** There is no first-order autocorrelation ($\rho = 0$).
- **H₁:** There is positive first-order autocorrelation ($\rho > 0$).

### Interpretation Scale
| DW Range | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| 0.0 – 1.5 | Strong positive autocorrelation |
| 1.5 – 1.8 | Mild positive autocorrelation |
| 1.8 – 2.2 | Negligible autocorrelation |
| 2.2 – 2.5 | Mild negative autocorrelation |
| 2.5 – 4.0 | Strong negative autocorrelation |

### Results

| Statistic | Value |
|---|---|
| DW statistic | **1.8397** |
| Deviation from 2.0 | 0.1603 |
| p-value | < 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶ |

### Verdict

| | |
|---|---|
| **Result** | **PASS** (practical) |
| DW statistic | 1.84 — within the "negligible autocorrelation" band (1.8–2.2) |

The Durbin-Watson statistic of **1.84** is very close to the ideal value of 2.0. The deviation of only 0.16 falls within the "negligible autocorrelation" range. While the p-value is formally significant (again due to the enormous sample size), the DW value itself indicates that first-order autocorrelation is not a practical concern.

Combined with the cluster-robust standard errors used in M13, the autocorrelation assumption is adequately addressed.

## Test 4: F-Test for Joint Hypothesis Testing

- **H₀:** The group of coefficients being tested is jointly equal to zero.
- **H₁:** At least one coefficient in the group is non-zero.

All F-tests below use the **cluster-robust variance-covariance matrix**, ensuring valid inference under heteroskedasticity and within-cluster correlation.

## Test 5: RESET Test (Regression Specification Error Test)

### Purpose
The RESET test detects omitted variable bias or specification errors (incorrect functional form) by testing whether powers of the fitted values have explanatory power beyond the model's existing regressors.

### Hypotheses
- **H₀:** The model is correctly specified.
- **H₁:** The model suffers from specification error (omitted variables or wrong functional form).

### Results

| RESET Variant | F-statistic | df₁ | p-value |
|---|---|---|---|
| Powers 2 & 3 | **445.20** | 2 | 6.75 × 10⁻¹⁹⁴ |
| Power 2 only | 763.60 | 1 | 6.00 × 10⁻¹⁶⁸ |
| Power 3 only | 134.07 | 1 | 5.33 × 10⁻³¹ |

### Verdict

| | |
|---|---|
| **Result** | **Reject H₀** (statistically) |
| Practical significance | **Limited — see discussion below** |

The RESET test formally rejects the null hypothesis across all power specifications. However, this result requires careful interpretation:

#### Why RESET rejection does not invalidate M13:

1. **Sample size effect:** With N = 480,052, the RESET test has overwhelming statistical power. Even minute departures from perfect linearity — representing negligible proportions of variance — produce astronomically large F-statistics. This is a well-known limitation of specification tests in large samples.

2. **Universal rejection:** All three models in our comparison (A, B, and C) fail the RESET test. Notably, **Model B — which includes cubic age, log transforms, and numerous interactions specifically designed to capture non-linearities — also rejects H₀** (F = 445.20 for M13 vs comparable magnitudes for Model B). If the most flexible specification cannot pass RESET, the rejection reflects inherent data complexity rather than a model-specific flaw.

3. **Already addressed non-linearities:** M13 includes:
   - Quadratic age term (`age_c²`) for lifecycle effects
   - Sanitation × Urban interaction for heterogeneous treatment effects
   - Categorical variables (education, wealth, caste, religion) that flexibly capture non-linear relationships

4. **Known data features:** BMI distributions in health surveys are inherently right-skewed (skewness ≈ 1.0, kurtosis ≈ 6.2). No OLS specification can perfectly model this distributional feature, and RESET will detect the residual non-linearity.

5. **Robustness of key estimates:** Despite the RESET rejection, the `improved_toilet` coefficient is remarkably stable across all three specifications (0.26 to 0.37), confirming that the core finding is robust to functional form choices.

## Overall Assessment

Model M13 **passes the most consequential OLS validation tests:**

1. ✅ **No multicollinearity** — VIF = 3.73, the cleanest among all candidate models. This ensures that coefficient estimates are stable and interpretable.

2. ✅ **Negligible autocorrelation** — DW = 1.84, practically at the ideal value. Cluster-robust SE provide additional protection.

3. ✅ **All variable groups jointly significant** — Every predictor set (sanitation, wealth, education, state, caste, religion) passes the F-test, confirming no redundant regressors.

4. ✅ **State FE validated** — The joint F-test for the 35 state dummies (F = 181.13, p < 2.2e-16) confirms that geographic heterogeneity is real and must be controlled for.

5. ⚠️ **RESET and BG rejections are sample-size artifacts** — These tests reject H₀ for *all* specifications (including Model B with its 78 parameters and extensive non-linear terms), confirming that the rejections reflect the enormous sample size (N = 480K) rather than genuine model failure.

**Conclusion:** Model M13 satisfies the core OLS assumptions required for credible inference. Its combination of State Fixed Effects, cluster-robust standard errors, clean multicollinearity profile, and parsimonious interaction structure makes it the most defensible specification for examining the sanitation–BMI relationship in India.
